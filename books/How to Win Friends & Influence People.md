A great example of what the book preaches is the title. Once you see it, you will never miss it again. How to Win Friends & Influence People practices thinking about the wants and desires of the readers.
# How to get most out of reading this book?
1) Have "*a deep, driving desire to learn, a vigorous determination to increase your ability to deal with people*" (Chant to yourself "My popularity, my happiness, and my sense of worth depend to no small extent upon my skill in dealing with people").
2) The first pass should be read quickly. The second time, go through it thoroughly.
3) Stop often and think. Ask how and when to apply.
4) Read and pull out sentences.
5) We forget what we don't use often. Keep going back and referring to it often.
6) Turn words into action. Do instead of think.
7) Put some stakes on actively applying the principles. Make it a game.
8) Do self-reflection of mistakes, successes (and actions to improve upon), and lessons.
9) Record your successes for your own benefit. List details.

# Part One: Fundamental Techniques in Handling People
## Chapter 1: "If You Want to Gather Honey, Don't Kick Over the Beehive"
- Principle 1: Don't criticize, condemn, or complain (Reward sympathy, tolerance, and kindness).
	- Reasoning
		- B. F. Skinner found that rewarding good behavior is a better teacher than punishing bad behavior. I think of coach Bill Belichick's approach to bad losses, when he said, "We're on to Cincinnati." That approach was great.
		- When Confucius said, "Don't complain about the snow on your neighbor's roof when your own doorstep is unclean," he meant that you should focus on what you can change: the way you interact with others.
		- I've found that the idea that "it takes character and self-control to understand and forgive" isn't easy to practice. You have to learn to let go and take time before you respond, not to mention that this is more for yourself than for the other person. A desire to understand and forgive can and should be cultivated.
	- Otherwise
		- We often forget that "criticism is futile because it puts people on the defensive and usually makes them strive to justify themselves," even though it is 100% true. Think of all the times we start to justify ourselves when people take things the wrong way. However, there is a difference between constructive criticism and outright bashing.
	- Notes
		- What is often forgotten is that it's human nature to criticize, condemn, or complain, which makes it hard to reward sympathy, tolerance, and kindness. Nevertheless, nothing worth accomplishing is easy, and in the end, it is worth the work.
		- Constructive criticism is either a sign of the strongest bonds or comes from having a deep connection.
		- Converting desire and care into action (drawing on the idea of excellence from *The Way of Excellence*) is vital to success in the age of AI. Think about all the tools at your fingertips; yet the paradox of choice (think of the famous jam study) suggests that people are better off with fewer options.

## Chapter 2: The Big Secret of Dealing with People
- Principle 2: Give honest and sincere appreciation.
	- Reasoning
		- We can only get people to take action "by making the other person want to do it," and therefore, "the only way I can get you to do anything is by giving you what you want." This follows simple logic.
		- Sigmund Freud said that everything we want falls under two motives, the sex urge and the desire to be great, so it follows that "the desire for a feeling of importance is one of the chief distinguishing differences between humankind and the animals." We can reframe these two motives as survival/pleasure and seeking status. Think about the way we usually grade: we have a baseline, and then an above and beyond.
		- William James said, "The deepest principle in human nature is the craving to be appreciated," and Carnegie adds, "If some people are so hungry for those feelings of importance and attention that they actually find solace in madness to win them, imagine what miracle you and I can achieve by giving people honest appreciation this side of sanity." Combining the two, we can use human nature to benefit ourselves, our relationships, and, in turn, others. We are often taught that we should be independent, yet we should make sure that independence is not mistaken for isolation.
		- We learn that Charles M. Schwab was valued for his ability to connect with people. He said, "I consider my ability to arouse enthusiasm among my people, the greatest asset I possess, and the way to develop the best that is in a person is by appreciation and encouragement," and "There is nothing else that so kills the ambitions of a person as criticisms from superiors. I never criticize anyone. I believe in giving a person incentive to work. So I am anxious to praise but loath to find fault. If I like anything, *I am hearty in my appreciation and lavish in my praise*." What's noteworthy here is that he is sincere. It's not flattery, and it's a habit: he has made it part of who he is by practicing it over and over (*Atomic Habits*).

## Chapter 3: "He Who Can Do This Has the Whole World with Him. He Who Cannot Walks a Lonely Way"
- Principle 3: Arouse in the other person an eager want.
	- Reasoning
		- I understand why self-help and self-improvement books are popular when they say, "the only way on earth to influence other people is to talk about what *they* want and show them how to get it." However, think about how much knowledge is in the world. Most of us have the tools to do anything we want to do, yet we don't. It's an investment of time and money.
		- We need to be reminded that "tomorrow you may want to persuade somebody to do something. Before you speak, pause and ask yourself: 'How can I make this person want to do it?'" I think the most important piece of this is that we need to take time to understand. We need to listen. We need to be patient. In the world today, this is easier said than done. Everything seems to hinge on success being immediate, but successes are rarely overnight.
		- Henry Ford said, "*If there is any one secret of success, it lies in the ability to get the other person's point of view and see things from that person's angle as well as from your own*." First, you have to understand the other person. Then, you have to translate it to the situation. This is the same as academic writing. In an academic writing class I took at Harvard Extension School, we discussed at length that what students often miss is a summary or paraphrase of what an author said. What is important is that the author's perspective sets up the argument. You need to have a reasonable understanding of the topic before you can make a claim about it.
		- I think the idea that "the world is full of people who are grabbing and self-seeking, so the rare individual who unselfishly tries to serve others has an enormous advantage" is not necessarily wrong. However, I push back on the idea that we can't align our self-interest with improving others' lives and, therefore, the greater good. I think we can align, and we can learn to align, our own interests with the greater good because success often depends on the help we receive.
		- The idea that "the best way to motivate someone to do something for you is to show how it would benefit them, as well" is important because it aligns your own self-interest with helping others. The two are not mutually exclusive.
		- This is the best case of show, don't tell: "When we have a brilliant idea, instead of making others think it is ours, why not let them cook and stir the idea themselves?" In most cases, this is true. Yet this is often paired with not taking ownership later, which can lead to not getting credit.
	- Notes
		- What is important about this is that we are using our own agency to care and using the human nature of greed to our advantage. Think about how people suggest applying to jobs now: figure out a company's wants and needs, and find a solution.
		- If I renamed the entire book, I'd call it *How to Care About Others*: using our innate greed and individualism to grow and prioritize our relationships.
# Part Two: Six Ways to Make People Like You
## Chapter 1: Do This and You'll Be Welcome Anywhere
- Principle 1: Become genuinely interested in other people.
	- People are naturally self-interested, which is why "\[y\]ou can make more friends in two months by becoming genuinely interested in people than you can in two years by trying to get people interested in you." We like relationships where we give and receive in return.
	- We live in a society that proclaims individualism. Yet Alfred Adler wrote "\[i\]t is the individual who is not interested in his fellow men who has the greatest difficulties in life and provides the greatest injury to others. It is from among such individuals that all human failures spring." What he is saying here is that extremely individualistic people hurt other people and overall are not successful in life. In fact, people seeking success often believe that being individualistic will make them successful, when the opposite is true.
	- The world, despite our best attempts to act without reciprocation, will always be run by the "you scratch my back, and I'll scratch yours," or, as Dale Carnegie wrote, "I have discovered from personal experience that one can win the attention and time and cooperation of even the most sought-after people by becoming genuinely interested in them."
	- Dale Carnegie wrote "\[a\]ll of us, whether we be workers in a factory, clerks in an office, or even a queen upon her throne: All of us like people who admire us." Two of the greatest examples are kids and pets, because they look up to us: kids to adults, pets to their owners.
	- In a world where everything is optimized, we need to relearn that "\[i\]f we want to make friends, let's put ourselves out to do things for others—things that require time, energy, selflessness, and thoughtfulness." But be careful of the other extreme: seeing the world as unchangeable.
	- Like a dog, "\[i\]f we want to make friends, let's greet people with animation and enthusiasm." This is useful to practice every day.
	- Dale Carnegie wrote "\[a\] long time ago, a hundred years before Christ was born, a Roman poet, Publilius Syrus, remarked '\[w\]e are interested in others when they are interested in us.'" The truth is that we like people who like us, those who can both receive and return effort. Be careful of perfectionism, because even good people—people who are actively working on being better—can be bad at receiving and returning effort in certain circumstances.
## Chapter 2: A Simple Way to Make a Good First Impression
- Principle 2: Smile.
	- People forget that "\[a\]ctions speak louder than words, and a smile says, 'I like you. You make me happy. I am glad to see you.'" Little acts build over time.
	- I agree that "\[y\]ou must have a good time meeting people if you expect them to have a good time meeting you." In almost all scenarios, there's an insecurity that causes a person to feel unworthy and therefore avoid society. However, there are really only two options. Either self-isolate or attempt to grow and learn.
	- William James wrote "\[a\]ction seems to follow feeling, but really action and feeling go together; and by regulating the action, which is under the more direct control of the will, we can indirectly regulate the feeling, which is not. Thus the sovereign voluntary path to cheerfulness, if our cheerfulness be lost, is to sit up cheerfully and to act and speak as if cheerfulness were already there." What James is saying is that we can only control what's within our control: our actions. However, this doesn't mean we should let things be the way they are, because that leads to always staying the same. The people worth following believe that staying the same is bad. Growth is a mindset that is vital in order to live a great life. If anyone says otherwise, don't follow.
## Chapter 3: If You Don't Do This, You Are Headed for Trouble
- Principle 3: Remember that a person's name is to that person the sweetest and most important sound in any language.
	- Dale Carnegie wrote "Jim Farley discovered early in life that the average person is more interested in his or her own name than in all the others on earth put together. Remember that name and call it easily, and you have paid a subtle and very effective compliment. But forget it or misspell it—and you have placed yourself at a sharp disadvantage." What's important to understand here is that you should consider others and that you should be careful about being wrong.
	- Dale Carnegie wrote "\[w\]e should be aware of the *magic* contained in a name and realize that this single item is wholly owned by the person with whom we are dealing...and nobody else. The name sets the individual apart; it makes him or her unique among all others. The information we are imparting or the request we are making takes on a special importance when we approach the situation with the name of the individual." What this reminds me of is *The Name of the Wind*. What is important is how the name embodies a person, although a name isn't as unique as he claims, since many people share the same name.
## Chapter 4: An Easy Way to Become a Good Conversationalist
- Principle 4: Be a good listener. Encourage others to talk about themselves.
	- Dale Carnegie wrote "\[b\]ut I had listened intently. I had listened because I was genuinely interested. And he felt it. Naturally that pleased him. That kind of listening is one of the highest compliments we can pay anyone." What is important is the "genuinely" piece. It is hard to fake actual interest but easy to spot someone looking for something.
	- Dale Carnegie wrote "\[b\]ut in reality what he wanted was a feeling of importance. He got this feeling of importance at first by kicking and complaining. But as soon as he got his feeling of importance from a representative of the company, his imagined grievances vanished into thin air." Understanding where to draw the line between bending the knee, so to speak, and using it strategically is where this theory breaks down a bit. It is hard to figure out where the line is.
	- Dale Carnegie wrote "\[s\]o if you aspire to be a good conversationalist, be an attentive listener. To be interesting, be interested. Ask questions that other persons will enjoy answering. Encourage them to talk about themselves and their accomplishments." A great conversation will always be two people trying to get the other to speak about themselves.
	- Dale Carnegie wrote "\[r\]emember that the people you are talking to are a hundred times more interested in themselves and their wants and problems than they are in you and your problems." This is almost always true. I see it in politics and, generally, with money.
## Chapter 5: How to Interest People
- Principle 5: Talk in terms of the other person's interests.
	- Dale Carnegie wrote "\[f\]or Roosevelt knew, as all leaders know, that the royal road to a person's heart is to talk about the things he or she treasures most." It is therefore important to understand that you will never change a person's heart, but rather you can help them see that they can change theirs.
	- Dale Carnegie wrote "\[t\]he most fascinating conversationalist that you know doesn't try to impress you with their knowledge of birding in Cornwall or bore you with the finer details of his or her daughter's upcoming nuptials even though they are undoubtedly well-versed in these topics. You enjoy their company because they talk in terms of *your* interests and opinions, and who isn't pleased by that?" What is important is figuring out what the other person likes and then actually talking about what they like. Surface-level conversations will eventually tire you out and leave you without feeling a connection.
	- "If you also use this, it will pay off for both parties. The other person will relish talking to you, but you as well will receive a reward: the enlargement of *your* life each time you speak to someone." People forget that by choosing to listen and be interested, you gain something too. You gain knowledge, you gain that person as a friend, someone willing to do things for you, and you gain the virtue of trying to be a good person.
## Chapter 6: How to Make People Like You Instantly
- Principle 6: Make the other person feel important-and do it sincerely.
	- "If we are so contemptibly selfish that we can't radiate a little happiness and pass on a bit of honest appreciation without trying to get something out of the other person in return-if our souls are no bigger than sour crab apples, we shall meet with the failure we so richly deserve." Learn to appreciate without expecting something in return. It is paradoxical but this is actually how you get something in return.
	- "And the pathetic part of it is that frequently those who have the least justification for a feeling of achievement bolster their egos by a show of tumult and conceit which is truly nauseating." Learn to leave your ego at the door. Learn to listen and understand another to truly connect with others.
	- "When she asked him fro his recipe for making these women fall in love with him, he said it was no trick at all: All you had to do was to talk to a woman about herself. The same technique works equally well with men." Falling in love is easy. The hard part is choosing to be with a person that you are willing to do this for over and over again.
