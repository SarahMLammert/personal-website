This is my favorite book of all time, hands down. I've read countless books in my journey to be a strong and influential writer and this is the more advanced version of They Say, I Say.
# Highly Effective Ethical Communication
- "*Effective communicators convey information how the human mind is wired to receive it*"
- "*Use a-rational biases to reinforce rational conclusions*"
- The main idea of the book is that "*\[c\]ommunication is not only an art - it's also a science.* This book is about that science. Most tomes on the topic are not." What Peter means is that there are specific formulas and patterns and not generic advice like the following:
	- "*Smile*"
	- "*Prepare*"
	- "*Be authentic*"
	- "*Keep it simple*"
	- "*Dress to impress*"
	- "*Speak from the heart*"
	- "*Show your audience why you care*"
	- "*Speak in terms of their interests*"
	- "*Emotion persuades, not logic*"
	- "*Speak with confidence*"
	- "*Overcome your fears*"
	- "*Respect is returned*"
	- "*Know your stuff*"
	- "*Be clear*"
- In answering whether it's ethical to use rhetoric, Peter says "\[r\]hetoric is a *tool*," one that we can use to inspire positivity or negativity. One of the two is ethical. If you use rhetoric to sow division, the problem is you, as Peter so easily put it. What's interesting to me about this is that this claim can be applied to guns and often is. Second amendment advocates often use the idea that guns are tools as a way to say that only bad people use guns to kill others. However, I think the stronger case is that the entire purpose of guns is to shoot something, inherently dangerous and negative. Now I understand if there is an immediate threat or wildlife, yet most of us are rational human beings and there are other ways of protecting ourselves too.
- "Highly effective people are highly effective communicators"
	- "A vague vignette"
	-  "A high-level overview"
	-  "One that isn't exhaustive"
- "Highly effective people get what they want and want what is good; *they make good things happen in good ways*"
- We can build our rhetorical toolkit using behavior economics where "\[b\]ehavioral economics is the field of psychology that reveals the landscape of our *cognitive biases*. A cognitive bias is a pattern in how we react to information, form judgements, and make decisions. An intellectual instinct, basically." 
- We have many cognitive biases that are shortcuts to conserve energy when judging. Peter describes these biases as necessary because we can't be debating all the time about every little piece of information. That is why trusting experts and science generally can be helpful. That is not to say that experts or science will always be useful or helpful or even right. Yet, people forget that individual experience rarely accounts for the distribution. Think like you are average and that the world relies on averages.
- "*Cognitive biases are how we think when we aren't thinking very hard - which is, and has to be, most of the time*"
- "*Learn the strategies, then become the strategies"

	- "*Move listeners toward good and rewarding choices*"
	- "*Help wisdom, excellence, and virtue go mainstream*"
